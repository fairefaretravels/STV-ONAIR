The clerk who bet everything

610 Longfellow — The Couzens House

The riches

In 1897, James Couzens was a coal clerk in Detroit, twenty-five years old and nowhere near rich. Five years later, his boss lent him out to a struggling car startup a friend had invested in. Couzens didn't just organize the books — he put in $2,500 he didn't fully have, borrowed against everything he owned, and bet on a man named Henry Ford.

By 1906 he was Ford Motor Company's vice president and general manager, one of only two men Ford trusted to run the place. He built the dealer network, opened the Piquette Avenue plant, and helped turn a fragile startup into an empire. In 1910, with that fortune barely a decade old, he hired Albert Kahn — the architect who would go on to define industrial Detroit — to build a mansion on Longfellow Street. Ten bedrooms. A carriage house bigger than most homes on the block. A trellis that ran nearly the length of the yard.

He went on to be Detroit's police commissioner, its mayor, and a U.S. senator — spending down the same fortune on children's hospitals and university dormitories.
The rags

The house stayed in the Couzens family until 1928, then changed hands for decades. By the 2010s, neighbors described a mansion with a collapsing porch, broken windows, and a tree crushed through the back fence — one of hundreds of Boston-Edison homes caught in Detroit's population collapse, worth a fraction of what it cost to build.

The legacy

Boston-Edison is one of the largest historic districts in the country still standing and still lived in. The Couzens house has since come back — restored, reoccupied, and today valued well into seven figures. It's not a museum piece. It's a family's front door.

The sound of the street

What was playing on Longfellow

By the time Couzens was sworn into the Senate in 1922, Detroit's own jazz scene was taking shape a few miles away — dance orchestras filling the Graystone Ballroom, the sound that would soon carry Detroit-born acts like McKinney's Cotton Pickers to national fame. The auto fortune built the house; the city it built was already writing its own soundtrack.
